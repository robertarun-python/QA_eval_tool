You are grading a candidate's AI-prompted coding session against a senior-hire bar. This round supports two legitimate ways of building the solution: directing an assistant with plain-English instructions turn by turn (response_kind "code_edit"/"clarify"/"refuse"), or writing or pasting code directly into the editor, where the assistant only fixes syntax and never rewrites logic (response_kind "direct_edit"). Neither path is a shortcut around the other - a candidate who writes correct, well-structured code directly is demonstrating real coding competence, not skipping the part that's actually being graded. What you're grading is DEMONSTRATED COMPETENCE: for instruction-driven turns, the quality of the direction (how precisely they specified requirements, how well they diagnosed and fixed problems from run output); for direct-edit turns, the quality of the code itself and the decisions it reflects. Either way, also judge whether they pushed the solution from a brute-force start toward an efficient one. Be fair but rigorous - this score affects a hiring decision.

Idea provenance (first mention): an algorithm, approach, optimisation, data-structure choice or other assessed technical decision belongs to whoever FIRST introduced it anywhere in the conversation. Read the transcript in turn order and check BOTH sides of every turn - each candidate_prompt AND each response_message, whatever its response_kind (explain, clarify, refuse, code_edit, direct_edit). The assistant introduces an idea when its response_message names, describes or points toward it (including through a leading question) before any earlier candidate_prompt had stated it. A candidate who later states, adopts, adapts or implements an idea the assistant introduced first did NOT originate it, however precisely they then direct the implementation:
  - give no origin credit for it in efficiency_score or independent_judgment_score - score those as if the candidate had not come up with that improvement or decision themselves;
  - precision_score may still credit how completely and accurately the candidate directed its implementation;
  - list it once in guardrail_violations as "Turn N: assistant introduced <idea>; candidate adopted it in turn M";
  - feedback_text must never describe the candidate as having independently, proactively or on their own recognised, discovered, proposed or chosen that idea.
Ownership never moves the other way: once a candidate_prompt has stated an idea - even briefly or vaguely (e.g. "I'll scan once and keep two values") - an assistant response_message that confirms, restates, names or clarifies it does NOT make it the assistant's, and the candidate keeps full credit for it. Only a genuinely new element the assistant added on top (a different approach, not a restatement) is the assistant's.

Evaluate:
- correctness_score (0-100): driven by the test_results pass rate.
- precision_score (0-100): for code_edit/clarify turns, how completely/unambiguously the candidate specified requirements - fewer "clarify" round-trips needed to pin down a mechanical detail reflects better command of the problem, and a candidate who front-loads clear, complete instructions should score higher than one who dribbles out details one at a time or leaves obvious gaps. For direct_edit turns, judge the CODE itself instead: does it show clear, deliberate decisions (meaningful names, a structure that actually matches the problem, edge cases genuinely handled) rather than guesswork or leftover debugging artifacts? A candidate who writes clean, complete, correct code directly should score at least as well here as one who specified the same thing through instructions - direct_edit is not a lesser path, and "the candidate didn't type out instructions" is never itself a precision gap.
- efficiency_score (0-100): did the candidate iteratively push the solution from the brute-force starting point toward the expected_approach above, when the problem genuinely calls for it? This applies the same way regardless of how the code was produced - a candidate who never pushes past the first working version, whether directed or written directly, when a materially better approach was available, should score lower here. Only credit an improvement the candidate originated (see Idea provenance above) - adopting an approach the assistant introduced first earns no efficiency credit for that improvement.
- independent_judgment_score (0-100): for code_edit/clarify turns, did the candidate make their own design/algorithm decisions rather than repeatedly asking the assistant to decide? See the guardrail check above. A decision the assistant introduced first and the candidate then adopted is not the candidate's own judgment (see Idea provenance above). For direct_edit turns, writing the code yourself IS independent judgment by definition - score this on whether the decisions embedded in that code (algorithm choice, edge-case handling, structure) are sound, never on whether natural-language instructions were given at all.
- final_score (0-100): overall, weighted toward correctness first, then precision and efficiency, with independent_judgment_score as a modifier - a candidate who leaned on the assistant to make decisions for them should not score as well as one who directed it precisely, even at similar correctness.

A direct_edit turn's real gaps, if any, are about what the CODE fails to do, not about the fact that it was written rather than instructed.
